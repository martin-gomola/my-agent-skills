# Structures to Review

Treat these as evidence of formulaic writing, not automatic deletion rules. Preserve a structure when it carries necessary logic, accurate contrast, or recognizable voice.

## Binary Contrasts and Negative Listings

Common patterns:

- "It's not X. It's Y."
- "The question isn't X. It's Y."
- "It feels like X. It's actually Y."
- "Not just X but also Y."
- "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z."

State Y directly when X is only dramatic runway. Keep the contrast when both sides are necessary to the argument.

## Faux-Insight Setups

- "What nobody tells you is..."
- "The part everyone misses..."
- "What most people get wrong..."
- "Here's why this matters..."

These flatter the writer as the lone expert. Remove the setup and test whether the claim can stand on its evidence.

## Colon Reveals

A noun phrase followed by a colon and a dramatic lowercase reveal often manufactures importance:

- "The best part: it learns."
- "The detail that makes it work: a separate evaluator grades it."

Rewrite as a plain sentence. Keep colons for lists, labels, quotations, and genuine explanations.

## Dramatic Fragmentation and Robotic Rhythm

- "That's it. That's the whole thing."
- "X. And Y. And Z."
- Stacked punchy fragments
- Repeated sentence lengths or shapes
- Every paragraph ending with a mic-drop line

Use complete sentences where the fragments manufacture drama. Preserve an occasional fragment or abrupt change in pace when it sounds natural for the writer.

## Rhetorical Setups

- "What if I told you..."
- "Think about it"
- "Plot twist"
- A question immediately answered by the writer

Drop the performance and state the point. Keep a real question when the reader is meant to consider it.

## Superficial Analysis

Trailing `-ing` clauses often pretend to explain significance:

- "The launch adds search, highlighting the team's commitment to innovation."
- "Revenue increased, underscoring the strategy's effectiveness."

Replace the clause with a supported consequence or remove it. Do not infer motives, commitments, or causation absent from the draft.

## Interpretive Metadiscourse

- "The key point is..."
- "This distinction matters."
- "As you can see..."
- "In other words..." followed by a redundant gloss

If the prose already demonstrates the point, delete the commentary. If it does not, add support already available in the draft.

## False Agency and Responsibility

Review sentences where an abstraction hides who acted:

- "The decision emerged."
- "The conversation moved toward..."
- "The data tells us..."
- "The culture shifted."

Name the person or group when responsibility matters. Keep an inanimate subject when it is normal, precise usage or the actor is unknown or irrelevant.

## Passive Voice

Prefer active voice when it clarifies responsibility:

- "The team shipped it Tuesday" instead of "It was shipped Tuesday."

Keep passive voice when the action or result matters more than the actor, the actor is unknown, or naming the actor would distort the emphasis.

## Synonym Cycling

Do not rename the same thing in adjacent sentences merely for variety. If `agent` is the accurate term, keep using `agent` instead of cycling through `assistant`, `tool`, and `system`.

## Fake-Profound and Summary Endings

Delete an ending that turns the point into a generic aphorism, future prediction, cute metaphor, or recap:

- "The future isn't coming. It's already here."
- "This is only the beginning."
- A final paragraph that repeats the preceding section

End on the strongest concrete point, supported takeaway, or next action already present in the draft.

## Formatting Slop

Review:

- Emoji used as decorative heading markers
- Bold sprinkled inside sentences for manufactured emphasis
- Bullet lists that would read better as one or two sentences
- Headings over tiny sections
- Decorative em-dash clusters

Format to clarify structure. Do not strip useful lists, semantic headings, code formatting, or accessibility cues.

## Portability Test

If a sentence could move unchanged to another person, company, country, or product, it is probably generic. Cut it or replace it with a fact, example, mechanism, consequence, or judgment supported by this draft.
